Section I. Abstract
Abstract (Draft – to be finalized later)
This experiment investigated free-stream velocity, Reynolds number, and vortex-shedding behavior in a modular wind tunnel using flow visualization and video-based measurements. Free-stream velocity was obtained by tracking glycerin-mist streaks in high-speed video, from which Reynolds number was computed using a defined characteristic length and air properties at room temperature. Vortex-shedding frequency was extracted from wake videos of a circular cylinder using both Fourier transform and autocorrelation methods, and the Strouhal number was calculated to assess scaling behavior. The measured vortex-shedding frequency increased approximately linearly with free-stream velocity while the Strouhal number remained within the expected range for subcritical flow, indicating consistency with classical vortex-shedding theory. Future work includes refining uncertainty estimates through improved imaging conditions and extending measurements to additional geometries and Reynolds number regimes.
Section II. Theory  
The free-stream velocity ​ is the primary experimentally controlled parameter in the wind tunnel and serves as the basis for computing dimensionless flow quantities such as the Reynolds number or Strohoul number. When tracer particles are sufficiently small and possess negligible inertia relative to the surrounding fluid, they follow the local fluid motion with minimal slip. Under this assumption, the displacement of visible tracer streaks over a known distance and time interval provides a direct estimate of the local flow velocity. This approach relies on the premise that the observed streaks accurately represent the free-stream motion and are not significantly affected by particle lag, gravitational settling, or diffusion.
The measured free-stream velocity is used to characterize the flow regime through the Reynolds number,

The Reynolds number provides a dimensionless framework for comparing flow behavior across different velocities and length scales and determines whether viscous effects or inertial effects dominate the flow dynamics.
For flow past a body, such as a circular cylinder, increasing Reynolds number leads to the development of an unsteady wake characterized by alternating vortex shedding. This phenomenon arises from flow separation on opposite sides of the body and results in a periodic oscillation of the wake. Section III. Experimental Methods
Experiments were conducted in a modular, open-loop wind tunnel with an adjustable fan to generate a range of steady free-stream velocities. The flow was lightly seeded with glycerin mist to produce visible streaklines while minimizing particle loading and disturbance to the flow. Videos of the seeded flow were recorded using a smartphone rigidly mounted outside the test section to ensure a fixed field of view. Recordings were taken at 240 frames per second, with this high frame rate beneficial to improve temporal resolution. Illumination was provided using controlled lighting to enhance streak visibility and contrast.
A calibration target consisting of two high-contrast marks separated by a known distance (L) was affixed to the exterior wall of the test section, centered vertically and aligned with the flow direction. This target provided a spatial reference for converting pixel displacement into physical distance. Free-stream velocity measurements were obtained at five distinct fan settings. For each setting, individual tracer streaks were tracked frame by frame as they traversed the calibrated distance. The frame numbers corresponding to the upstream and downstream calibration marks were identified, and the elapsed time was determined from the frame difference and recording frame rate. Multiple streak measurements were performed at each fan setting to obtain a mean free-stream velocity and assess measurement variability.
To investigate vortex shedding, a circular cylinder was mounted at mid-span within the test section and centered vertically to reduce end effects and asymmetries in the wake. The cylinder length was measured directly and used as the characteristic length for Strouhal number calculations. 5 wake videos were recorded at 3 selected fan speeds, under 2 separate lighting conditions corresponding to previously measured free-stream velocities, while maintaining consistent camera positioning, framing, and frame rate to ensure comparability across trials.
Recorded image sequences were converted to grayscale and processed to extract a time-resolved brightness signal from specified regions of interest located downstream of the cylinder. 4 regions were selected to capture the alternating vortex structures in the near wake, far wake, narrow region, and wide region, to compare and balance spatial averaging and signal contrast. The vortex-shedding frequency was determined using both Fourier transform analysis over the brightness time series, which identifies dominant frequency peaks in the spectral domain, and autocorrelation analysis, which quantifies periodicity in the time domain. These two methods were compared over the 2 lighting configurations and 4 region-of-interest definitions, and evaluated over their influence on signal quality, frequency clarity, and repeatability, enabling identification of optimized measuring conditions for subsequent measurements.
Following optimization lighting, region-of-interest configuration, and vortex shedding frequency classification,  the selected conditions were held fixed for subsequent measurements. Two additional objects were installed individually at mid-span within the test section, and their characteristic dimensions were measured. For each object, wake videos were recorded at three selected fan speeds corresponding to previously measured free-stream velocities. Vortex-shedding frequencies were extracted using the same analysis procedures, and the resulting data were used to compute Reynolds and Strouhal numbers for comparison across objects.
